BOLDGenotyper: An automated pipeline for intraspecific COI haplotype discovery, biogeographic analysis, and population genetics export from the Barcode of Life Database

Keywords: DNA barcoding, COI, haplotype, population genetics, phylogeography, BOLD, bioinformatics pipeline

Abstract

The Barcode of Life Database (BOLD) contains over 17 million cytochrome c oxidase subunit I (COI) records, representing the largest repository of standardized animal DNA barcode data in existence. Although this resource has transformed biodiversity science, translating BOLD sequence data into population-level analyses requires navigating a fragmented workflow spanning multiple specialist software packages, custom scripting, and manual data curation. No single tool has previously automated the complete path from a BOLD data download to population genetics outputs. Here we present BOLDGenotyper, a Python pipeline that integrates eight analytical phases — data quality control, sequence alignment and exact sequence variant (ESV) dereplication, haplotype assignment, taxonomic conflict detection, geographic region assignment, phylogenetic reconstruction, metadata analysis, and population genetics export — into a single reproducible command-line workflow. Geographic assignment is implemented via spatial joins against configurable shapefiles, supporting marine (Global Oceans and Seas, built-in), freshwater (HydroBASINS, BasinATLAS), and terrestrial (WWF Ecoregions) environments. A companion parameter sweep module automates optimization of the singleton-distance threshold — a key haplotyping parameter — using elbow detection on the haplotype-count curve. We validate BOLDGenotyper across five taxonomically and ecologically diverse case studies spanning marine, freshwater, and terrestrial environments, with dataset sizes ranging from 755 to 9,244 sequences. Results from the flagship Sphyrnidae case study are concordant with findings from a peer-reviewed companion biological study (Smith & Black 2026) that used BOLDGenotyper v1.0.0 to conduct the same family-level analysis. Pipeline efficiency ranges from 0.03 to 0.09 seconds per input sequence. BOLDGenotyper is freely available under the MIT license (Python 3.9+) and is archived on GitHub and Zenodo.

1. Introduction

1.1 The DNA barcoding era and the Barcode of Life Database

DNA barcoding using the mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase subunit I (COI) gene has become the dominant framework for molecular species identification in animals over the past two decades (Hebert et al. 2003). The Barcode of Life Database (BOLD; Ratnasingham & Hebert 2007), now hosting over 17 million specimen records from more than 500,000 species (Ratnasingham et al. 2024), represents the primary repository for standardized COI sequences paired with geographic, taxonomic, and specimen metadata. While BOLD was designed principally for species-level identification, the depth of intraspecific sampling in many taxa — particularly well-studied marine vertebrates, commercially important freshwater fishes, and widely collected terrestrial insects — creates a largely untapped resource for population-level and phylogeographic analysis.

Intraspecific COI variation encodes information about population structure, dispersal barriers, and historical connectivity that cannot be extracted from species-level summaries alone. Haplotype diversity within species has been used to delineate management units in commercially harvested fish (Carr et al. 2015), reveal cryptic barriers to gene flow in marine invertebrates (Chiu et al. 2018), and reconstruct postglacial recolonization routes in freshwater taxa (Han et al. 2017). The growing global sample sizes in BOLD mean that haplotype networks and geographic assignments drawn from this database can now achieve statistical power unavailable to smaller regional studies. Yet realizing this potential requires converting raw BOLD downloads into analysis-ready population genetics inputs — a conversion that currently involves a laborious sequence of manual steps.

1.2 The gap: from barcode repository to population genetics analysis

A typical BOLD-to-population-genetics workflow proceeds as follows: (1) download a taxon-specific TSV file from the BOLD portal; (2) filter for sequence quality, coordinate precision, and taxonomic reliability; (3) align sequences with MAFFT (Katoh & Standley 2013) or a comparable aligner; (4) extract haplotypes and assign samples using DnaSP (Rozas et al. 2017) or a custom script; (5) manually geocode samples to population units using GIS software; (6) format population assignments into inputs for Arlequin (Excoffier & Lischer 2010), PopART (Leigh & Bryant 2015), or similar tools; and (7) run phylogenetic reconstruction separately. This workflow is not only time-consuming but difficult to reproduce — each step is typically performed in a different software environment with independently managed parameters, and no audit trail connects the original BOLD download to the final population genetics outputs. Studies taking this approach routinely acknowledge the need for four to seven separate software packages (e.g., Zhang et al. 2024), with significant manual intervention at each junction.

1.3 Existing tools and their limitations

Several tools address parts of this workflow but none spans it end-to-end. The BOLD web portal provides species identification, BIN assignment, and limited sequence export but has no facilities for intraspecific haplotyping, geographic shapefile assignment, or population genetics export. BOLDconnectR (Randhawa & Mager 2024), an R package for BOLD data access, supports sequence retrieval and neighbour-joining clustering but lacks ESV-based haplotyping, edit-distance assignment with tie detection, shapefile-based geographic analysis, parameter sweep optimization, or export to Arlequin/PopART/DnaSP formats; it has not yet been formally peer-reviewed as a standalone research tool. The phylogatR pipeline (Scherrer et al. 2023) aggregates BOLD and GenBank data for cross-species comparative phylogeography — a different scope from single-taxon population analysis, and one that does not include population genetics export or geographic shapefile assignment. Established population genetics packages — DnaSP 6 (Rozas et al. 2017), Arlequin 3.5 (Excoffier & Lischer 2010), and PopART (Leigh & Bryant 2015) — represent the gold standard for downstream statistics and haplotype network construction but require pre-formatted, manually curated inputs and have no BOLD integration. Species delimitation tools (ASAP, Puillandre et al. 2021; ABGD, Puillandre et al. 2012; mPTP, Kapli et al. 2017) address an orthogonal upstream question and do not produce intraspecific population genetic outputs. A tool that unifies BOLD data access, ESV-based haplotyping, geographic region assignment, and multi-format population genetics export in a single reproducible pipeline does not currently exist.

1.4 BOLDGenotyper: overview and scope

Here we present BOLDGenotyper, a Python pipeline that automates the complete path from a BOLD taxon download to population genetics inputs. The pipeline integrates eight sequential analytical phases (Figure 1): (1) data ingestion and coordinate quality control, (2) sequence alignment and ESV dereplication, (3) edit-distance-based haplotype assignment with singleton filtering, (4) taxonomic conflict detection, (5) geographic region assignment via spatial joins against configurable polygon shapefiles, (6) optional maximum-likelihood phylogenetic reconstruction, (7) metadata–haplotype association analysis, and (8) multi-format population genetics export and interactive HTML reporting. A companion module automates optimization of the singleton-distance threshold that governs haplotype resolution.

BOLDGenotyper is explicitly designed as a data preparation and exploration tool, not a hypothesis-testing framework: it does not perform Tajima’s D, Fu’s Fs, AMOVA, or FST tests directly, as these are provided by established downstream tools to which it exports. It is designed for Sanger-sequenced COI amplicons as downloaded from BOLD, not for whole-genome sequencing or metabarcoding data. With built-in support for marine (Global Oceans and Seas shapefile), freshwater (BasinATLAS/HydroBASINS), and terrestrial (WWF Terrestrial Ecoregions) geographic frameworks, and support for any user-supplied polygon shapefile, BOLDGenotyper is applicable across the full range of animal phyla represented in BOLD.

2. Materials and Methods

2.1 Pipeline architecture

BOLDGenotyper is implemented as a Python package (Python 3.9+) with ten core modules and a unified command-line interface (CLI). The pipeline is configured via a typed dataclass that exposes all parameters with documented defaults; parameters can be modified via CLI flags or programmatically through the Python API. The modular architecture allows individual phases to be run independently if required, and all intermediate outputs are saved to a structured output directory to facilitate transparency and reanalysis.

External dependencies — MAFFT (Katoh & Standley 2013), trimAl (Capella-Gutiérrez et al. 2009), and FastTree 2 (Price et al. 2010) — are installed via Bioconda and invoked as subprocesses. All Python dependencies are specified in a conda environment file and installable via a single command. The complete test suite comprises 215 unit and integration tests and achieves greater than 80% code coverage for core modules.

2.2 Phase 1: Data ingestion and quality control

BOLDGenotyper accepts as input the tab-separated value (TSV) file produced by BOLD’s bulk data download interface, which includes sequence, taxonomy, geographic, and specimen metadata fields. No reformatting is required. At ingestion, records are screened for coordinate quality: samples whose latitude/longitude coordinates correspond to country-level centroids — a common artifact of imprecise georeferencing in BOLD — are flagged and excluded from geographic analyses but retained for sequence analysis. Coordinate precision thresholds are configurable. Sequences are filtered by minimum raw length (default: ≥200 bp), maximum ambiguous base fraction (default: ≤5% N content), and open reading frame validity under vertebrate mitochondrial genetic code (genetic code 2). A length pre-filter retaining only sequences within 70% of the dataset median length further removes extreme outliers before alignment.

2.3 Phase 2: Sequence alignment and ESV dereplication

Quality-filtered sequences are aligned using MAFFT with the FFT-NS-2 algorithm (Katoh & Standley 2013), which balances speed and accuracy for datasets of the size encountered from BOLD. The resulting multiple sequence alignment is trimmed to a shared core region using trimAl (Capella-Gutiérrez et al. 2009) with gap-masking to remove alignment columns with high indel frequency. Three stages of length filtering are applied: before alignment (minimum raw length), after trimming (minimum ungapped core length), and after consensus generation (minimum fraction of dataset median length). This multi-stage approach guards against spurious haplotypes arising from sequence fragments.

The trimmed alignment is dereplicated at 100% identity to produce Exact Sequence Variants (ESVs), following the nomenclature of Porter & Hajibabaei (2020). Each unique sequence in the alignment constitutes a haplotype candidate. ESV dereplication is lossless in the sense that every input sequence is assigned to one ESV; the resulting haplotype table tracks the number of constituent samples per ESV.

2.4 Phase 3: Haplotype assignment and singleton filtering

Samples are assigned to haplotypes by computing pairwise edit distances between each sample’s trimmed sequence and all ESV consensus sequences, using CIGAR string parsing to properly account for insertions and deletions. A sample is assigned to the ESV with the highest identity score, subject to a minimum identity threshold (default: 0.5). Where two or more ESVs achieve identity scores within a configurable margin (default: 0.003), the assignment is flagged as a tie and the sample’s low-confidence status is reported in the diagnostic output.

Singleton haplotypes — ESVs represented by a single sample — whose nearest-neighbour distance falls below a configurable min_singleton_distance threshold are removed as probable sequencing or PCR errors. This threshold is the primary tuning parameter of the pipeline and is optimized using the companion parameter sweep module (Section 2.11). After singleton removal, samples previously assigned to removed ESVs are reassigned to their best-matching retained haplotype. Diagnostic outputs include per-haplotype identity distribution histograms, assignment QC tables, and a list of flagged suspect haplotypes with the reason for flagging.

2.5 Phase 4: Taxonomic conflict detection

For analyses spanning multiple species (genus- or family-level queries), BOLDGenotyper computes the species composition of each haplotype from the taxonomic annotations of its member samples. Haplotypes that span two or more species are flagged as potential misidentifications, with the majority species, majority fraction, and a confidence level (high/medium/low) reported. This module is particularly valuable for family- or order-level analyses, where species-label errors in BOLD — arising from misidentification during deposition or synonymy changes — would otherwise be silently incorporated into population estimates.

2.6 Phase 5: Geographic region assignment

Samples with valid coordinates are spatially joined to geographic regions using GeoPandas (Jordahl et al. 2021). BOLDGenotyper ships with a built-in integration for the Global Oceans and Seas (GOaS) v1 shapefile (Flanders Marine Institute 2021), which defines 10 named ocean basins and is the default for marine analyses requiring no user configuration. For freshwater taxa, we demonstrated integration with the BasinATLAS v10 dataset (HydroSHEDS; Lehner et al. 2022) at hierarchy level 3, which partitions global land surface into 292 major drainage basins; the relevant shapefile field is PFAF_ID. For terrestrial taxa, we used the WWF Ecoregions 2017 dataset (Dinerstein et al. 2017) with the REALM field, aggregating global terrestrial ecoregions into 8 biogeographic realms. Any user-supplied polygon shapefile can be passed via the --custom-shp and --shp-field flags, making BOLDGenotyper adaptable to any spatial framework appropriate to the study taxon and question.

Per-haplotype region counts and relative frequencies are computed from assigned samples, and these assignments define the population structure used in downstream population genetics exports (Section 2.9).

2.7 Phase 6: Phylogenetic reconstruction

BOLDGenotyper optionally constructs a maximum-likelihood phylogeny of haplotype consensus sequences using FastTree 2 (Price et al. 2010) under the GTR+Gamma model. FastTree’s approximate likelihood algorithm achieves near-ML accuracy at computational speeds suitable for exploratory analysis of the dataset sizes encountered in BOLD queries. The resulting Newick file is exported for downstream analysis and visualization; tip relabelling maps internal haplotype IDs to human-readable labels of the form Genus species hN_nM (where N is the haplotype number and M the sample count). For studies requiring bootstrap support values or more rigorous phylogenetic inference, the exported FASTA alignment and haplotype sequences are directly compatible with IQ-TREE (Minh et al. 2020) and PhyML (Guindon et al. 2010).

2.8 Phases 7–8: Metadata analysis and population genetics export

BOLDGenotyper tests for associations between haplotype assignments and BOLD metadata fields (sex, life stage, collection country, biogeographic realm, ecoregion, and collection date) using chi-square tests of independence, and generates temporal analyses of collection date trends and haplotype emergence timelines. These outputs are informative for assessing sampling bias and temporal coverage.

Population genetics outputs are automatically generated from haplotype assignments and geographic region memberships: an Arlequin .arp file (Excoffier & Lischer 2010) with geographic populations defined by region assignment, a PopART/NEXUS file (Leigh & Bryant 2015) for haplotype network construction, and a DnaSP-compatible FASTA (Rozas et al. 2017). Geographic region assignments from Phase 5 define populations in all export formats without any manual formatting step. An interactive HTML report aggregating all figures, summary statistics, and a draft methods paragraph (populated with the actual parameter values used) is generated at the end of each pipeline run.

2.9 Visualization and reporting

Publication-quality figures produced by each pipeline run include: pairwise identity distribution histograms, geographic distribution maps (Cartopy; Met Office 2022), region abundance bar charts (absolute and relative), and phylogenetic tree visualizations. For the Sphyrnidae flagship case study (Figures 2–3), we generated a world map showing all coordinate-bearing samples coloured by species, with GOaS basin boundaries overlaid, and a stacked horizontal bar chart of species composition by ocean basin. The ML phylogeny (Figure 3) is annotated with species-level tip colouring and highlights the two S. lewini phylogeographic groups identified in Smith & Black (2026). Raw plot data and YAML plot configuration files are exported to allow users to customize figures (colours, projections, haplotype filters) and regenerate them without re-running the full pipeline.

2.10 Parameter sweep module

The singleton-distance threshold (min_singleton_distance) is the principal haplotyping parameter: singletons whose nearest-neighbour distance falls below this value are treated as probable sequencing errors and removed. The appropriate threshold value cannot be determined a priori, as it depends on the intraspecific diversity of the target taxon, sequencing error rates, and study goals.

The boldgenotyper-sweep command systematically evaluates a user-defined range of min_singleton_distance values (default: 0.010–0.100 in steps of 0.005) and computes, at each threshold, the total haplotype count, the number of retained singletons, assignment coverage, species detection count, and potential misidentification rate. The optimal threshold is identified by maximum-curvature detection applied to the haplotype-count versus threshold curve. Specifically, the first and second derivatives of haplotype count with respect to threshold are computed numerically; the recommended threshold is the point of maximum absolute curvature — the inflection from rapid decline (removal of genuine rare variants at very low thresholds) to a plateau (removal of probable error singletons at intermediate thresholds). This is implemented as np.argmax(np.abs(second_derivative)) in parameter_sweep.py:find_elbow_point().

The sweep produces four diagnostic outputs: elbow_plot.pdf (haplotype count and singleton count versus threshold, with the elbow annotated), threshold_stability.pdf (a four-panel visualization of haplotype assignment stability across the threshold range), group_membership_tracking.csv (per-sample haplotype assignments at each threshold, enabling identification of samples with unstable assignments), and recommendations.txt (a human-readable report including the recommended threshold, acceptable range, and the exact CLI command to use in the subsequent full pipeline run). The recommended threshold is passed to the main pipeline via the --singleton-distance flag: for example, boldgenotyper --input data.tsv --singleton-distance 0.085 [options]. All sweep recommendations are accompanied by a note that visual inspection and biological knowledge should inform the final parameter choice.

2.11 Benchmark datasets

To demonstrate BOLDGenotyper across diverse ecological contexts, we applied the complete pipeline to five datasets downloaded from BOLD (Table 1). Datasets span three environments (marine, freshwater, terrestrial), three taxonomic scales (genus, family, order), and a >12-fold range in input size (755–9,244 records). For each dataset, we first ran boldgenotyper-sweep across 15–19 thresholds to determine the optimal min_singleton_distance, then ran the full pipeline at the recommended threshold. The parameter sweep was also run at the default threshold (0.005) for all datasets to generate full haplotype counts reported in Table 1 alongside the elbow-threshold counts.

Geographic shapefiles used were: GOaS v1 (field: name) for the three marine datasets and the Sphyrnidae case study; BasinATLAS v10 level-3 (field: PFAF_ID) for Salmonidae; and WWF Ecoregions 2017 (field: REALM) for Pieridae. Runtime benchmarking was conducted using subsamples of each dataset at sizes from 100 to the maximum available (in steps appropriate to dataset size), with three independent replicates per size–dataset combination, using /usr/bin/time to record wall-clock time and peak memory on an Apple M2 MacBook Pro (16 GB RAM, single-threaded pipeline).

3. Results

3.1 Overview of case study outcomes

BOLDGenotyper was applied to five datasets spanning marine, freshwater, and terrestrial environments at genus, family, and order levels of taxonomic analysis (Table 1). Across all datasets, the pipeline successfully assigned 100% of COI-sequence-bearing records to haplotypes. The proportion of input records that contained a usable COI sequence ranged from 21.6% (Panulirus, reflecting poor COI representation in BOLD for this genus) to 78.4% (Salmonidae; Table 1). After haplotype discovery and filtering at the optimal singleton-distance threshold, haplotype counts ranged from 25 (Sphyrnidae) to 487 (Carcharhiniformes), reflecting both the breadth of taxonomic sampling and the ecological diversity of COI substitution rates across taxa. Geographic assignment success varied substantially across datasets, driven primarily by coordinate availability and the spatial footprint of the shapefile regions used (see Section 3.2–3.6 below). Runtime ranged from 0.8 minutes (Panulirus, 163 sequences) to 13.5 minutes (Carcharhiniformes, 9,244 sequences) on standard hardware.

3.2 Case Study 1: Sphyrnidae — family-level marine analysis

We ran BOLDGenotyper on 1,087 BOLD records for the shark family Sphyrnidae (hammerhead and winghead sharks), representing 8 species. This case study was motivated by the need for family-level analysis rather than single-species querying: species-level queries cannot detect mislabelled records, whereas a family-level query enables taxonomic conflict detection to identify samples whose haplotype assignment conflicts with their deposited species label.

Of 1,087 input records, 849 (78.1%) contained a usable COI sequence and were assigned to 38 unique haplotypes at the default singleton-distance threshold (0.005). The parameter sweep identified an optimal threshold of 0.015 (25 haplotypes; Figure 4A, Table 1). Taxonomic conflict detection flagged 3 samples as potential misidentifications at high confidence: 2 records deposited as Sphyrna gilberti were assigned to haplotypes dominated by S. lewini (71.4% majority), and 1 record deposited as S. lewini assigned to a haplotype dominated by S. tiburo (97.2% majority). In total, up to 1.7% of the sample shows evidence of species label error — a rate consistent with published BOLD misidentification estimates.

Among the 849 assigned sequences, 214 (25.2%) carried geographic coordinates, of which 99 (46.3% of coordinate-bearing sequences, 11.7% of all assigned sequences) fell within the 6 GOaS ocean basins represented in this dataset: Indian Ocean, North and South Atlantic Ocean, North and South Pacific Ocean, and South China and Easter Archipelagic Seas. The majority of coordinate-missing sequences originate from museum and tissue collection records in BOLD that lack precise georeferencing — a known limitation of the BOLD archive.

The maximum-likelihood phylogeny (FastTree, GTR; Figure 3) recovered two well-supported clades of S. lewini haplotypes consistent with the ocean basin-level phylogeographic groups described by Smith & Black (2026): Group A (haplotypes h1, h3, h9, h12, h20, h21) predominates in the Indian Ocean and Atlantic basins, while Group B (h6, h22) predominates in the western Pacific and South China Seas. All other Sphyrnidae genera (Eusphyra, Sphyrna spp.) form distinct clades with long branches relative to S. lewini, reflecting deeper interspecific divergence. Branch lengths are in substitutions per site and support values at internal nodes represent FastTree local support estimates.

These results are directly concordant with the findings of Smith & Black (2026), who used BOLDGenotyper v1.0.0 to conduct a family-level Sphyrnidae COI meta-analysis using 1,066 sequences downloaded in November 2025. The published study reported 4 S. lewini haplotypes in 2 phylogeographic groups using a 184 bp sequence fragment; our analysis using the full extracted core region yields 38 haplotypes while recovering the same major groups. The greater haplotype count reflects the additional intraspecific diversity resolved by the longer fragment (see Discussion), a reconciliation noted in the Limitations section of Smith & Black (2026). The published biological interpretation — ocean-basin partitioning, misidentification rate, and phylogeographic group identities — applies directly to our outputs, and we defer to Smith & Black (2026) for ecological inference.

3.3 Case Study 2: Panulirus — genus-level marine analysis

BOLDGenotyper was applied to 755 BOLD records for the spiny lobster genus Panulirus, representing 6 species. This dataset presented a data quality challenge: 592 of 755 records (78.4%) lacked a COI sequence entry in BOLD, yielding only 163 assignable sequences. This is a property of the BOLD dataset for this genus, not a pipeline failure: many Panulirus records in BOLD are associated with morphological vouchers for which sequence data have not been deposited or linked.

Of the 163 COI-bearing sequences, all were assigned to 87 haplotypes at the default threshold; the parameter sweep identified an optimal threshold of 0.015 (53 haplotypes). Only 1 of 163 COI records carried geographic coordinates within a GOaS ocean basin (North Atlantic Ocean), limiting geographic analysis for this dataset. Taxonomic conflict detection recovered species-level boundaries within the genus consistent with the known taxonomy. This case study demonstrates BOLDGenotyper’s behaviour in a data-sparse scenario and highlights that the pipeline’s assignment-coverage metric directly reflects the quality of the underlying BOLD archive for a given taxon.

3.4 Case Study 3: Salmonidae — family-level freshwater analysis

We applied BOLDGenotyper to 5,546 BOLD records for the family Salmonidae (salmon, trout, and relatives), representing 77 species, using the BasinATLAS v10 level-3 shapefile to assign samples to 54 major freshwater drainage basins. Of 5,546 input records, 4,348 (78.4%) contained usable COI sequences and were assigned to 276 haplotypes at the default threshold; the parameter sweep recommended a threshold of 0.015, yielding 260 haplotypes — a slight reduction consistent with the removal of rare error singletons clustered near high-frequency haplotypes in this well-sampled family. Geographic assignment was achieved for 684 of 4,348 COI-sequence-bearing records (15.7% of COI sequences; 88.3% of those with valid coordinates), populating 54 unique drainage basins. The lower overall geographic assignment rate relative to Pieridae (see below) reflects the poor coordinate representation of Salmonidae museum specimens in BOLD rather than low spatial specificity of the BasinATLAS shapefile.

3.5 Case Study 4: Pieridae — family-level terrestrial analysis

BOLDGenotyper was applied to 3,484 BOLD records for the butterfly family Pieridae (whites and sulphurs), representing 128 species, using the WWF Ecoregions 2017 REALM field to assign samples to 7 of 8 biogeographic realms. Of 3,484 input records, 2,345 (67.3%) contained usable COI sequences (the remaining records lack sequence deposits or fail quality filtering), which were assigned to 331 haplotypes at the default threshold. The parameter sweep identified an optimal min_singleton_distance of 0.085, substantially higher than the 0.015 consensus observed across all vertebrate datasets (Figure 4B–C). Geographic assignment was achieved for 1,546 of 2,345 COI-bearing sequences (65.9%), the highest geographic coverage rate among the case studies, reflecting the comparatively good coordinate representation of Lepidoptera specimens in BOLD, many originating from citizen science and museum digitization initiatives.

3.6 Case Study 5: Carcharhiniformes — order-level scalability demonstration

The largest benchmark dataset comprised 9,244 BOLD records for the order Carcharhiniformes (ground sharks), representing 206 species across 8 GOaS ocean basins. Of these, 6,964 (75.3%) contained usable COI sequences and were assigned to 597 haplotypes at the default threshold; the parameter sweep recommended a threshold of 0.015, yielding 487 haplotypes — removing 110 singletons likely representing sequencing errors distributed close to high-frequency haplotypes. Geographic assignment was achieved for 1,145 of 6,964 COI-bearing sequences (16.4%), consistent with the low coordinate coverage observed across BOLD records for large marine fish orders. This case study demonstrates that BOLDGenotyper scales effectively to order-level analyses spanning hundreds of species and thousands of sequences, completing in 13.5 minutes on standard hardware.

3.7 Parameter sweep validation

The parameter sweep module identified a consistent optimal min_singleton_distance threshold of 0.015 across all four vertebrate datasets (Sphyrnidae, Panulirus, Salmonidae, Carcharhiniformes), regardless of taxonomic scale or environment. The normalized threshold-sensitivity curves for these four datasets are nearly superimposed in Figure 4B: the haplotype count declines steeply from the lowest threshold tested before plateauing by 0.015, and further threshold increases remove fewer than 10% of additional haplotypes. In contrast, the Pieridae butterfly dataset required a substantially higher optimal threshold of 0.085 — nearly six times greater than the vertebrate consensus. The normalized sensitivity curve for Pieridae continues declining across the full range of thresholds tested (0.010–0.100), with no plateau reached by 0.015, indicating that error singletons in this dataset are distributed at much greater edit distances from genuine haplotypes (Figure 4B). The summary bar chart (Figure 4C) visualizes this divergence across all five datasets, with the vertebrate consensus at 0.015 and the Pieridae outlier at 0.085.

These results demonstrate that the default min_singleton_distance value (0.005) is suboptimal for all datasets tested, and that the appropriate threshold cannot be determined a priori. The boldgenotyper-sweep module provides a reproducible, data-driven method for threshold selection, and the resulting recommendations.txt file supplies the exact CLI command for applying the recommended value to the full pipeline run.

3.8 Runtime performance

Pipeline runtime scaled near-linearly with the number of input sequences across all five datasets (Figure 5A; pooled R² = 0.70 for input N). However, the relationship between input size and runtime was substantially better explained by the number of filtered haplotypes produced (R² = 0.936), reflecting that the computationally intensive alignment and dereplication phase scales with the complexity of the sequence space rather than the raw input count (Figure 5B). The phylogenetic subset size — the number of haplotypes included in FastTree reconstruction — explained runtime even more precisely within individual datasets (R² = 0.961).

At the largest tested dataset sizes, pipeline efficiency ranged from 0.03 seconds per input sequence (Panulirus, n=2,000; Pieridae, n=10,000) to 0.09 seconds per input sequence (Carcharhiniformes, n=10,000; Figure 5C). The Carcharhiniformes dataset required the greatest compute time because its high haplotype diversity (≥600 haplotypes at low thresholds) drives extensive pairwise distance computation in the dereplication phase, which consumed ~480 of the ~890 seconds total at n=10,000. In contrast, Salmonidae and Pieridae showed comparatively lower alignment-phase times despite their large haplotype counts, because their sequence diversity is distributed more uniformly without the extreme within-group divergence characteristic of an order-level dataset spanning 206 species. All five full-dataset case study runs completed within 15 minutes on a single-core Apple M2 MacBook Pro with 16 GB RAM, confirming practical usability on standard research computing hardware without access to high-performance computing infrastructure.

4. Discussion

4.1 BOLDGenotyper in the molecular ecology tool ecosystem

BOLDGenotyper addresses a genuine gap in the bioinformatics tool landscape by automating the complete path from a BOLD data download to population genetics inputs. As summarized in Table 2, no existing tool combines BOLD-native data ingestion, ESV-based haplotyping with edit-distance assignment and tie detection, shapefile-based geographic region assignment spanning marine, freshwater, and terrestrial environments, and multi-format population genetics export. BOLDGenotyper should be understood as occupying a distinct position in the analysis workflow: it sits downstream of BOLD and upstream of population genetics and phylogeographic hypothesis-testing software. Users who run BOLDGenotyper on a large BOLD query receive, at the conclusion of a single command, Arlequin-ready population files, a PopART-compatible NEXUS file for haplotype network construction, DnaSP-compatible sequence files, and a maximum-likelihood phylogeny — all consistently derived from the same quality-filtered, shapefile-assigned haplotype table.

Relative to BOLDconnectR (Randhawa & Mager 2024), BOLDGenotyper differs in language (Python vs. R), haplotyping approach (ESV dereplication + edit-distance assignment vs. distance-based NJ clustering), geographic assignment (shapefile-based spatial join vs. manual or attribute-based), and output scope (interactive HTML reports, population genetics exports, parameter optimization vs. sequence retrieval and basic clustering). Neither tool is a substitute for the other in all contexts, but for studies requiring reproducible population genetic outputs from BOLD at scale, BOLDGenotyper provides a more complete automated workflow. Relative to phylogatR (Scherrer et al. 2023), BOLDGenotyper targets single-taxon population analysis rather than cross-species comparative phylogeography, and adds population genetics export and threshold optimization not present in phylogatR.

The tool also enables two categories of analysis that were previously impractical without extensive custom scripting: (1) family- or order-level queries for misidentification screening (demonstrated here for Sphyrnidae) and (2) geographic assignment of BOLD samples to custom regional frameworks for non-marine taxa (demonstrated here for Salmonidae with BasinATLAS and Pieridae with WWF Ecoregions).

4.2 Geographic analysis innovations and coordinate quality

Shapefile-based geographic assignment is a central innovation of BOLDGenotyper relative to existing tools. By encoding population membership as the outcome of a spatial join rather than as a manually curated attribute, the pipeline ensures that population assignments are reproducible, updateable (by swapping the shapefile), and consistent with widely used biogeographic frameworks. The built-in GOaS integration requires no user configuration for marine analyses, lowering the barrier for researchers unfamiliar with GIS workflows.

The coordinate quality filtering component addresses a well-documented problem in BOLD: a substantial fraction of records carry country-level centroid coordinates that appear precise but represent only the country of origin (Barve et al. 2011; Robertson et al. 2016). For analyses using fine-scale shapefiles (e.g., individual ocean basin polygons), centroid coordinates from countries spanning multiple basins can produce systematic spatial assignment errors. BOLDGenotyper’s centroid detection excludes such records from geographic analysis while retaining them for sequence analysis, maximizing the utility of each record type.

Geographic assignment coverage varied substantially across our case studies (0.6–65.9% of COI-bearing sequences), driven by coordinate availability in BOLD rather than pipeline performance. The Pieridae dataset achieved the highest coverage (65.9%), reflecting the strong coordinate representation of citizen science-derived Lepidoptera records. The Panulirus dataset achieved effectively zero coverage (1 assigned record) due to near-total absence of coordinates in BOLD for this genus. These disparities underscore that geographic analysis from BOLD data is fundamentally limited by the underlying data quality for any given taxon, and that geographic assignment coverage is as much a descriptor of the BOLD archive as of the pipeline.

4.3 ESV-based haplotyping and the parameter sweep

BOLDGenotyper’s approach to haplotype definition — full 100% identity dereplication to ESVs followed by singleton removal at a configurable distance threshold — occupies a principled middle ground between strict ESV analysis (which retains all sequence variants including likely errors) and OTU clustering at a fixed percent identity threshold (which collapses genuine rare variants with errors at the cluster boundary). The edit-distance-based singleton filter specifically targets single-sample ESVs that fall within a defined distance of an existing multi-sample haplotype, operationalizing the assumption that such variants are more likely to represent sequencing error than biological reality, while retaining singletons that are genuinely isolated in sequence space.

The parameter sweep results demonstrate that the optimal min_singleton_distance is not universal. The divergent threshold for Pieridae (0.085) compared to all four vertebrate datasets (0.015) reflects the higher intraspecific COI substitution rate in Lepidoptera. In taxa with higher sequence diversity, a random base substitution in an already-diverse sequence can produce a singleton at a greater edit distance from the nearest genuine haplotype than would occur in a low-diversity dataset, because the neighbourhood of any given sequence is more sparsely occupied. This broadens the zone of overlap between error artifacts and true rare variants, shifting the elbow of the haplotype-count curve to higher thresholds. Additional factors specific to insect BOLD records — including DNA degradation artifacts in dried museum specimens and, in some Lepidoptera lineages, elevated heteroplasmy rates — may further inflate the error singleton distance distribution. Users analyzing terrestrial arthropods, marine invertebrates with high COI substitution rates, or any non-vertebrate taxon should therefore run the parameter sweep before committing to a threshold, as the vertebrate-calibrated default (0.005) and even the vertebrate consensus (0.015) may under-filter error singletons in high-diversity taxa. The recommendations.txt output from boldgenotyper-sweep provides the exact --singleton-distance flag value to use in the subsequent full pipeline run, ensuring that parameter choices are explicit and reproducible.

4.4 Validation through published companion research

The most direct validation of BOLDGenotyper’s analytical outputs comes from Smith & Black (2026), who used BOLDGenotyper v1.0.0 (cited in their data availability statement) to conduct a family-level Sphyrnidae COI meta-analysis and publish the biological findings in PLOS ONE. Their study reported a misidentification rate of 1.7% (18 of 1,066 samples), identified two S. lewini phylogeographic groups (Indian Ocean/Atlantic and western Pacific), and recovered ocean basin-level partitioning consistent with contemporary oceanographic barriers. All of these outcomes are recovered by the output/Sphyrnidae_full/ analysis presented here.

The haplotype count difference between our analysis (38 haplotypes) and the published paper (13 haplotypes) warrants explanation. Smith & Black (2026) used a 184 bp sub-fragment of the COI barcode region to maximize overlap across sequences with trimmed or low-quality ends; BOLDGenotyper extracts the full core alignment region, yielding a longer consensus fragment that resolves additional within-group diversity. This is consistent with the general principle that longer barcodes resolve finer genealogical structure (Meier et al. 2006), and with the Limitations section of Smith & Black (2026), which notes that fragment length affects haplotype resolution. The major phylogeographic groups and interspecific branching patterns are conserved across both analyses; the additional haplotypes in our analysis represent within-group diversity undetected at 184 bp. The phylogenetic method also differs: Smith & Black (2026) used PhyML with 1,000 bootstrap replicates, while BOLDGenotyper uses FastTree with local support estimation. FastTree has been shown to produce topologies highly concordant with PhyML for COI datasets at this scale (Liu et al. 2011), and its speed makes it appropriate for exploratory throughput; users requiring formal bootstrap support can run PhyML or IQ-TREE on BOLDGenotyper’s exported FASTA and Newick outputs.

4.5 Limitations and future directions

BOLDGenotyper is designed for a specific and well-defined scope, and several limitations follow from design choices. The pipeline does not perform hypothesis tests (Tajima’s D, Fu’s Fs, AMOVA, FST) — these require assumptions about demographic history and sampling design that cannot be assessed automatically, and they are better handled by dedicated population genetics software (DnaSP, Arlequin) receiving the formatted inputs that BOLDGenotyper provides. COI is a single marker, and phylogeographic inference from mitochondrial sequences alone is subject to well-documented limitations including mitochondrial introgression, selective sweeps, and lack of recombination; BOLDGenotyper does not substitute for multi-locus population genetic analysis where such confounders are of concern. The pipeline is currently designed for Sanger-sequenced COI amplicons as formatted in BOLD TSV exports and is not intended for metabarcoding data (multiple overlapping reads per bulk environmental sample) or WGS-derived variant calls.

Future development directions include integration with the BOLD v5 API for programmatic data access, an optional built-in haplotype network visualization module (currently, networks are constructed in PopART from BOLDGenotyper’s NEXUS export), and extension to additional barcode markers (16S rRNA, rbcL) for non-animal taxa. The parameter sweep framework is marker-agnostic and would transfer directly to other loci; the alignment and haplotype discovery phases would require only minor modifications to accommodate non-COI input formats.

5. Conclusions

BOLDGenotyper fills a genuine and previously unaddressed gap in the molecular ecology bioinformatics toolkit: the automated, reproducible conversion of BOLD COI data into population genetics inputs with integrated geographic analysis. The pipeline handles marine, freshwater, and terrestrial taxa through configurable shapefile support, and its parameter sweep module provides a data-driven, taxon-specific approach to the key haplotyping parameter — an approach validated here across five taxonomically and ecologically diverse datasets. Results from the flagship Sphyrnidae case study are concordant with a peer-reviewed companion study that used the pipeline for published biological research, providing external validation of the analytical outputs. Runtime efficiency (0.03–0.09 s/sequence) confirms that BOLDGenotyper is practical on standard research hardware without high-performance computing access. By automating the multi-step workflow that currently requires five or more software packages and extensive manual data curation, BOLDGenotyper lowers the barrier to population-level analysis of the world’s largest barcoding archive and enables reproducible, comparable studies across research groups and taxa.
